Episode 1: People we want to empower



[A letter from one activist organization to another]



As I understand it, NAAME.org's mission is to "amplify the work of alternative radio, print, film, and strategic media makers in the Pacific Northwest", so I’ll focus on how Needpedia can do that, through an example. Bear in mind “ideas” could refer to anything from improving small details of an existing project, to creating an entire media collective.



For this example we’ve got a bunch of activists in Detroit building their own internet, (which is a real project by the way). A project this technical requires expertise with electronics, and involves complicated information that organizers are constantly having to re-explain. And each time they do so they’re limited by their ability to present information. But with a specialized archive for ideas like Needpedia, they could post about things once, and simply copy and paste links to that post from there. They no longer have to repeat themselves anymore.



While it’s already possible to do this using existing websites, you then have to update the website every time the discussion changes, and you have to contend with the prospect of people doubting your website’s legitimacy because they know it really just says whatever you want it to. But on Needpedia they know all criticisms can be shared, (in fact the “token system” lets you drop entire debates right after any word you want, embedded into little interactive tokens people can easily ignore if they want to). People also know that on an archive for ideas like Needpedia, that if there’s a better idea it’s really easy to share and see.



So if you get linked to an idea-post that’s gained lots of attention, and you see solid counter-arguments for each concern and doubt expressed about it, and you see no one’s been able to think of a better idea, then suddenly that link has accomplished what a simple website normally cannot. -It’s instilled confidence, because it’s not just some random idea, it’s the highest rated solution. -All while imparting a very large amount of highly relevant information, in addition to providing tools for easily acquiring more.



So Imagine the activists in Detroit listing their problems as problem-posts on Needpedia, then having the community share all their ideas for each problem. These posts are tagged so that activists, tech-enthusiasts, and people learning to code can all easily find them. -This is actually even better than it sounds because it unlocks human potential. In addition to organizers having better access to technicians, (and technicians having better access to projects that actually mean something to them), suddenly people who are in retirement, learning a craft, or are simply bored, (people who wouldn’t have gotten involved under normal circumstances), can find interesting projects in their area and abroad. A retired electrician in Canada does a search for idea posts with a “layer” for “Electricians”, and finds a post from the activists in Detroit. He sees an idea for powering relay stations using solar power, but they need technical assistance. (They created an empty layer for Electricians so that they could share links to them in social media groups related to electricians, but that didn’t pan out and instead one from Canada simply found them on their own, in this example).



The electrician adds technical information to the electrician layer, and user-friendly instructions, plus links to useful hardware, on the public layer. A few of the activists in Detroit enabled “tracking” on the idea post they made for solar powered relay stations, so the moment it got updated by the electrician, they got notified. Suddenly they have the technical information they needed, and a potential new contact for more. -Empowering them to work on new ideas, and emboldening the entire community.



Traditional consulting fees are prohibitively expensive, and a majority of intelligent people feel empty simply toiling away for businesses, so the fact activists get free technical guidance, and that experts get an easy and highly rewarding way to finally use their abilities to do something that directly changes peoples lives, and brings genuine gratitude and social cohesiveness, strongly suggests we have a sustainable model. The ‘cost-benefit’ relationship is advantageous for all parties involved. Additionally, programmers and artists often need work to build their portfolios, so large numbers of them could use Needpedia to empower activists globally.



Funding is also important to consider. Normally when someone considers donating, they do so hoping they can trust the organizers to use it wisely, which often leads potential donors to cynicism and doubt, which inhibits even the best of organizations. But once the electrician came and told everyone what they’ll need to buy, all other supporters can see that list and choose to buy and donate the items themselves. Which instantly nullifies any doubts about how a financial donation might be spent, and provides a level of satisfaction that excites people. Suddenly activism provides instant gratification, and a neighborhood’s got community controlled internet.  -Or simply a backup internet in case the government ever tries digitally blinding or silencing the public.



It’s also important to notice how much easier making change became for the activists in this scenario. Traditionally they’d have to knock on a bunch of doors or something to engage people in their area, constantly repeating themselves hoping to persuade them, as individuals. But with Needpedia they just had to post an idea and people came to them. -In practice it will always be wise to promote ideas by sharing links to them on other sites, with related groups and forums, but if enough people start using Needpedia, even a random idea some child had one day could grow into an international collective of citizens and experts, theoretically. Especially in games and other artistic works, which Needpedia can be used for as well.



In conclusion, Needpedia provides organizations with an archive for solutions to their problems that excites users and makes it easy for them to engage in extremely effective ways. On an archive for all ideas, good ideas stop looking ordinary or random, because suddenly it becomes clear no one’s been able to think of a better one. It contextualizes ideas and establishes a form of competition between users to create the best ones, which then benefits the public. Needpedia’s debate tokens let people on both sides rate arguments individually, which then makes it easy for everyone to understand people’s concerns better, which then enables them to create better compromises. -And to address concerns with greater accuracy, and detail. In other words, while some will essentially compete to create the highest rated ideas on Earth, others will find that even their boredom can lead to tangible social progress, because with the right tech it’s surprisingly easy, and because there’s few things more interesting than changing the world.



Episode 2: Reflecting on the work of doctorates



In the Tao of Conversation, Dr. Kahn compares the communication styles of seminar groups based on their overall efficacy. He begins with the group he calls ‘the beauty pageant’, these are comprised of individuals who seek only to have their own ideas win. Not only are they uninterested in which ideas can most benefit the whole, they will often mischaracterize the ideas of others in their own petty attempt to “win”. Dr. Kahn identifies a number of groups but for the sake of brevity I’ll skip right over to the one he identifies as the most ideal: the one he calls “barn builders”, which is inspired by observations of Amish communities. If a member of their community needs a barn, the whole community comes together and makes it happen. A barn building group is comprised of individuals who seek the greatest benefit to all. Not only will they patiently hear out everyone with an idea, they will actively work to improve each others ideas in the hopes that the group as a whole may benefit from the full potential of their collective knowledge and resources. They want everyone to win.



Anyone paying attention can tell which group they’d want to be a part of, and politically speaking, which group sounds the most familiar. And yet, as profound as that difference is, it is but a single insight compared to the work done by Dr. Rosenberg.



Rosenberg began his career working with violent offenders in prisons, and would eventually be called in to help resolve violent conflicts between tribes and other groups. His key insight is that once people stop believing it’s possible to solve their problems by communicating their needs, violence in one form or another, is inevitable. The barriers to such understanding are numerous, but fall within a remarkably small number of patterns. The most common being that people label one another instead of identifying the needs which compel others to act. They will also often employ loaded terminology, which justifies their own position. All wars begin with dehumanization, which inherently relies upon labels and implications. Rosenberg points out that the moment each side can describe the needs of the other side to the satisfaction of their opposition, that a solution is usually not more than 20 minutes away.



Needpedia is a mass conflict resolution technology partially inspired by Rosenberg’s NonViolent Communication. Not only because its layer system can be used to create layers for NVC, (wherein even the most controversial of discussions can be translated into NVC); but also because the very framework of Needpedia itself, inherently facilitates what Rosenberg calls “need literacy”. By listing all the needs in our communities we can forget whether someone is “conservative” or “liberal”. If you care about solving, say, homelessness, you click on it, and immediately begin discussion of potential solutions.



In line with Dr. Kahn’s work, each idea may be considered and refined with the collective insights of an unlimited number of minds. The token system will make it easy to ask questions, debate, and link to sources, while the layer system makes it easy to for experts to create their own spaces to deliberate with others in and out of their field. The layer system also makes it easy for communities to connect with experts, thereby catalyzing the formation of citizen-expert-coalitions. This promotes scientific literacy, and empowers students to learn from experts in the field. But most importantly, optimization infrastructures have the power to prove once and for all that coalitions of citizens and experts can outperform politicians. That a viable alternative is not only possible, it has finally arrived.




Episode 3: Time Banks

Time banks sound mysterious but they’re actually very simple. They let you trade efforts with a whole network of other people. If you want something done, you simply make a post about it in the time bank, state how many credits it’s worth, and see if anyone messages you about it.

To get credits you simply look up posts and help others in the network.

Even if you live in the middle of nowhere, you can still ask for help with things online.

This way Needpedia not only makes it possible to share ideas, it makes it easier than ever to get help with them.

Episode 8: How we win



It used to be, that if things ever got too bad, people would ban together and do something about it. Whether there was a mad king, or a flood, if it was bad enough people would ‘ban together’ to do something about it.



But things in the modern age have gotten complicated, often it’s nearly impossible to even agree on what needs to be done, let alone on what to do about it.



In America it’s even more hopeless thanks to the 2 party system. It’s not based on figuring out what the best ideas are for the nation, if anything it’s designed to pit the public against each other while benefiting the powerful.



Then there’s the way mass media sensationalizes stories, and often even lies in order to get clicks and ad revenue. And that’s on top of the natural complexity involved in truly understanding events, science, and people. There are so many problems it’s hard to imagine things getting better from here.

Most of these problems can’t be solved alone, and need to be addressed through governance, which, sadly, seems to almost never work. It can take years just to get something on the ballot.



Whether people want to work on homelessness or the military prison industrial complex, making a significant impact takes so long that by the time you succeed there’s 10 other problems. And that’s assuming you can accomplish changing a single thing.



That’s why we need better technology. We’ve got more problems, and thanks to the dysfunction of our governments and media, working on each one is way more complex. This is why we need a wiki for all ideas. We can come together over the problems that matter to us, pool knowledge inspiration and insights to develop each idea to its fullest, then pick the idea that makes the most sense to you, (sometimes more than one idea can be pursued at a time).



Thanks to Needpedia’s “Layer System”, which lets you add layers to idea posts to cover certain aspects of an idea, people can hear directly from experts themselves. The goal is to create a catalyst for citizen-expert coalitions. Suddenly important information won’t have to come from the media because when people hear about things and go to lookup solutions, the most current and cutting edge information will be available to them right there.

Needpedia also provides the token system, which lets users drop question tokens directly into bodies of text, which can be clicked on to open. Question tokens let you ask questions but we’ve already designed a few more. Debate tokens let you see the ratings of specific arguments, for people on both sides of a debate. This means everyone can see which concerns matter the most to everyone else, and can propose smarter compromises.



The heart of our current problem is the concentration of power into the hands of an elite few, so of course the antidote would be an open forum. Where our current decision making systems are slow and ineffective, a wiki for all solutions enables citizens and experts to connect in ways that have never even been seen before.

“Wiki” is the Hawaiian word for fast, the creator of wikis chose that name for them because he realized they would be one of the fastest ways for groups of people to share information.



How we win


Citizen-experts will have a lot of power because democracy is built on the idea that people matter, and with the help of experts there’s no telling what they’ll be able to accomplish. Especially if they’re organized in a highly effective way, with a resource like Needpedia.

In terms of governance, it may sound boring, but once we have a legally definable entity which can be used to create and display numerically quantifiable consensus, we can form legal contracts for representatives that do increasingly more powerful and important things to hold them accountable.

Needpedia lets you ‘quantify’ consensus, which makes it possible to contract for it.

The key point of failure for the invention known as a “politician” is that someone can bribe or threaten them. The same way a CPU can fail on a computer, the CPU of society, politicians, can have the economic pressure of millions, sometimes billions of human lives, bearing down upon them. It’s like trying to build a skyscraper on a foundation of cardboard, it’s just too much pressure for a group of people who are only human. And have families that can be targeted, politically or otherwise. If you could magically fix that one failure you would change the world.

So if citizen-expert coalitions can manage to actually change the things the politicians can’t control, like social movements, social spaces, and to some extent places like workplaces and schools, people are going to start sharing ideas for things on larger and larger scales, like cities and nations.

And once solutions are established, politicians ignoring them will look increasingly bad, people can run pressure campaigns using Needpedia, because it showcases ideas.

Then eventually we’ll hopefully have the power to fix the above mentioned point of failure. We don’t have magic, but with Needpedia we do have a legally definable entity, and those can be used to create contracts.

Communities can use those contracts to bind representatives who are willing to sign them. By signing them they get the support of communities, both during elections, and while they’re in office. With Needpedia’s ability to list all problems and ideas, communities can support leaders in ways that money literally can’t buy. Crowdsourcing solutions can be truly invaluable but having a whole movement of people who truly trust and support you is game changing. It means you don’t have to focus on fundraising with lobbyists to afford ads, to get votes. You already have them, and a community working to get you more.

With a contract, if we reach 70% agreement on an idea and a politician votes against it, we could hold them accountable, possibly even remove and replace them.



In any case, once citizens manage to find a few things they mostly agree on, Needpedia will make it surprisingly easy for everyone to see which politicians aren’t serving the interests of the public. Because the interests of the public are finally publicly archived. This itself begins to change everything,



And I mean everything. Not only will people be able to use Needpedia to address problems with things like holidays, and other concepts, but they’ll be able to talk about problems with corporations.



Whether a corporation, (or politician), likes it or not, if they’re a public entity, people can list all their problems publicly. Supporters can also add their ideas for those problems, ask questions, and debate. So politicians and corporations who listen to citizens will be rewarded and the ones who don’t will look bad. You could think of this as phase 1



Phase 2 is when we get representatives to sign contracts that penalize them for disobeying consensus. From there we work to make those contracts stronger while building community with citizens and experts. The goal is to prove to voters that properly equipped citizen-expert coalitions work, and to politicians that it’s important to voters that they agree to respect our decisions. -hopefully by signing contracts that guarantee it.

Politicians aside, the world is full of people who care about things, and experts who would like to help them. By creating an archive for all people’s ideas for all problems, we make it possible for people to help one another in countless new ways, and now that we’re done building all the most essential parts, we hope you’ll join us in taking the next steps.




Episode 9: Trusting Wizards



Science, has changed, everything. Just look around you. Our very DNA has already been changed by it, and we haven’t even scratched the surface of what’s possible yet. We could be on the verge of seeing people live in outter space, 3D print organs, and all kinds of other wild things, all thanks to science.



And yet most people don’t really care about science, or trust scientists. -if you merely talk about it too much they won’t like you. -Even though they can see people actively using it to literally fly through the air, and cure diseases.



To understand why, consider the difference between being able to trust an airplane, and being able to trust a vaccine. When you use an airplane, it’s not mysterious, you fly to your destination and that’s that. It might be bad for the environment but we all know planes work.



Now think about having someone inject you and your children with something you don’t understand, and cannot test. And as much as they say it’s done for your safety, you know complications can happen, and that they can be fatal.



It’s like if you had to go see a wizard every time you had a health problem. If you had no way to actually understand, test, or see what they were doing, you’d already be in a very awkward position. Now imagine it was technically possible for the wizards to make lots of money off of you if you’re sick. They swear they’re good, but you hear stories of all kinds of scary things.



Now imagine all the people who defend the wizards call you stupid for not trusting the papers published by the wizards. Which you think is just insulting until you realize they’re also talking about making laws to force everyone to let the wizards put things in their bodies. In your body, and the bodies of your family.



Hopefully that gives you an understanding of the fear and mistrust these people experience. It’s nothing like trying to trust an airplane.



‘With great power comes great responsibility”, and a responsibility I believe the scientific community has been failing in, is how much more seriously we all need to be taking scientific literacy. People don’t understand, we don’t need 20% or even 200% more of what we’re doing, we need to start doing things we’ve never even talked about doing before.



It’s not enough that scientists publish papers, those can be faked, so we need to create ways for people to test things for themselves. You could design an efficient public testing system, but if they have to mail in the sample or something, the whole project would just fail because skeptics would say ‘how do I know your lab results aren’t faked?’. You could build public testing facilities, but the moment they’d have to pass off their sample to a technician, or anyone else, they no longer know if the results they’re getting are from their sample. -At this point most people throw up their hands and say “see, these people are impossible” but the reality is that’s nothing more than a comfortable excuse to exit the discussion. It’s a cop out. We have a noble goal, (to govern by consent and transparency, instead of force or coercion), and it’s got a few design challenges. -Embrace that, and it’s actually not so bad. Especially if you have a good system for collaborating.



With Needpedia, entire communities can collaborate on proposals together, and list every single idea they have. In ordinary meetings after people say things, they can only hope that others heard them before their words vanish into the mists of time. But on a wiki for solutions, ideas stay, and begin taking on a life of their own.



For instance people in cities are starting to form biohacking labs and “fab labs” (where they fabricate all kinds of things using 3d printers), these are spaces where citizens get together to use science, and governments could support them to promote scientific literacy. The more the labs can do, the more people they’ll draw in. So by supporting local scientific organizations and enthusiasts, governments can make the equipment necessary to test samples more common and accessible. They also make it easier for people to prove claims wrong, so ordinary people will be equipped to disprove hoaxes and other nonsense.



Ideally we reach a point where people who are about to get a shot have the legal right to randomly stop the procedure, demand a sample, and take it to an independent lab where they can see what it’s made of for themselves. So that the next time someone creates a hoax that scars a bunch of parents into not vaccinating their kids, people those parents personally know, and others from local labs, would be posting their evidence to disprove the hoax and reassure parents. It’s not a perfect system but it would be far better than what we’ve got today.



What we’ve got today is basically just a flaming pile of trust issues. But with Needpedia communities on both sides of any issue can develop better methods for building trust, collaboratively.



Once there’s trust, you’d be surprised by what people can figure out. For example, if people are concerned about the toxins used to preserve vaccines maybe someone could produce a slightly more expensive option that’s based on refrigeration and quicker distribution. Or if people are afraid of injecting pieces of a deadly virus into their children maybe we can offer them antibody therapy instead.



With Needpedia’s Layer System everyone’s free to see what experts think about people’s ideas; and with the Token System, they can see everyone else’s doubts and questions about peoples ideas all throughout the site. The Layer System can also be used to create conflict resolution layers, where controversial issues like vaccines can be broken down into expressions of needs, (no insults allowed). That means nomatter how angry other people get about a subject, you have a place, a layer, where you can talk about it constructively with others. Tools like these are precisely why Needpedia has the power to make a difference. The world is full of serious problems, and scattered data; now it needs a wiki to organize people’s ideas, to let them see all their options, side by side.





Episode 10: The best it could possibly be



Frankly, it sucks that the word “governance” is so boring, because all the flashy stuff you see in movies, the explosions, uprisings, and other things, all happen -or not- as a result of governance. Whether or not your life sucks, is largely the result of governance. The problem is that by complicating things, it becomes possible to scam people, so over time, governments became the most boring things on Earth.



I think we all know governments could be better. They’re slow, expensive, and corrupt. At the end of the day, politicians are just a form of information technology that was invented before the age of toilette paper. -Or pencils. It’s primitive, our technology has advanced lightyears beyond the need for the vast majority of what we call “government” today.



In Athens people simply gathered on the side of a hill and talked about their problems. But that doesn’t work for large numbers of people, so people created “representative democracy”, a democracy where someone “represents” you. Unfortunately the truth is that politicians don’t fight for your interests, they serve the people you’re fighting against.



The sick, brutal truth of the world is that the fact you’re not paying someone to breathe right now is proof that someone’s not “making money”. Your freedom is someone else’s “lost economic opportunity”. People who sell prisons don’t make money when they help criminals end patterns of violence and addiction, so they don’t offer them. Many argue the system they’ve created actually hardens criminals, thereby actually endangering the public. In effect, they’re inherently anti-freedom. Every single company out there wishes it could have a monopoly and force people to pay as much as they want. Because ultimately, a company is really just a group of people who’ve decided to work together to get as much as they can, like a band of proud sociopaths. They do this by providing services and products so a lot of people worship them, but at the end of the day, it is more profitable to have people in jails, in debt, and working all the time, than it is to have freedom. We are their prey, they regularly pay think-tanks to outsmart us. Wage theft dwarfs the expense of all other forms of crime, and yet still remains legal. Because corruption is the gravity of governments. If a democracy was a airplane, the thing it’s always resisting in order to not crash, is corruption. When corruption gets bad we end up with fascism, where simply expressing dissent can get you killed. All governments are influenced by money, and all democracies demand things like fair wages and the right to protest, therefore, democracies must be built to protect human rights and the environment the way airplanes are built to survive gravity. It’s fun making analogies but I’d like to do something equally fun, but infinitely more productive: ask what is the best that it could possibly be?



The whole point of a government, really, is just to figure out what the biggest problems are, then what the best ideas are for solving those problems. It only makes decisions that’s it, so the best a government could possibly be, would involve citizens and experts listing all the problems they know about, then listing all their ideas for solving them. On a fundamental level, it’s really that simple, governments don’t actually need politicians. -or even buildings. It could literally just be citizens and experts, on a website.



By using the net we make it easy for people to participate whenever they want, from wherever they want, but that’s only the beginning. Online we can alter the very texture of communication itself.



Imagine you’re in an auditorium listening to a famous speaker sharing her ideas for improving something in your city, and thousands of other people have come to listen. If the speaker says something you wanted to comment on, question, or debate, you wouldn’t have many options.
