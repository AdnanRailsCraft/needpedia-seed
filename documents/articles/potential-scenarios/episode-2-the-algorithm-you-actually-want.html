Episode 2: The Algorithm You Actually Want

Taking Back Your Feed

The Doomscroll

It's 11 PM. Marcus has been scrolling for two hours. He can't remember when he picked up his phone or why.

His feed is:

  • Outrage about a politician he can't vote against

  • Someone's perfect vacation (he can't afford one)

  • An argument about something that happened three years ago

  • A video designed to make him angry

  • An ad for something he doesn't need

  • Another argument

  • More outrage

  • Someone's engagement ring (reminder he's single)

  • A "news" article that's actually rage bait

He feels worse than when he started. Anxious. Angry. Inadequate. Powerless.

He knows it's designed this way. The algorithm learned that negative emotions keep him scrolling. More engagement = more ad revenue. His mental health is the product being sold.

But what's the alternative? Stop using social media entirely? Miss everything happening in his community, lose touch with friends, become isolated?

There has to be another way.

The Alternative

His friend mentions it: "You ever check out Needpedia's social media section? It's... different."

Marcus is skeptical. Every new social platform promises to be "different" and ends up exactly the same—because the business model is always the same. Keep people scrolling. Sell their attention.

But Needpedia is open source. Non-profit. No investors demanding growth at any cost.

He creates an account.

The first thing he sees isn't a feed—it's a choice.

"What do you want to see?"

Not what the algorithm thinks will keep him scrolling. What he actually wants to see.

Building Your Own Algorithm

The interface shows him options:

Feed Types:

  • Solutions Near You - Projects being implemented in your area

  • Projects Needing Your Skills - Tasks matching your abilities

  • Topics You Follow - Updates on ideas you've subscribed to

  • Community Updates - People working on things you care about

  • Progress Tracking - Objectives being completed on projects you follow

  • Learning - Explanations and discussions on subjects you want to understand

  • Connections - People with complementary skills or shared interests

He can mix and match. Prioritize what matters. The algorithm serves him instead of manipulating him.

Marcus starts simple: "Solutions Near You" + "Projects Needing My Skills"

His feed transforms.

What He Actually Sees

Post 1: Community garden project three blocks from his apartment needs someone with graphic design skills for a grant proposal. He's a graphic designer. There's a task card with exactly what they need, timeline, and how to volunteer.

Post 2: The homeless shelter he passes every day has an idea for expansion—they've got 60% of objectives complete, just need help with permit applications and some carpentry work.

Post 3: A local teacher created a free tutoring program and needs volunteers. Schedule shows evening and weekend slots. He could actually do this.

Post 4: The intersection near his building—the dangerous one where someone died last year—there's an active project to get traffic calming measures. They're coordinating a city council presentation next month and need people to show up.

Post 5: Someone started a tool library in his neighborhood. Borrow equipment instead of buying it. They need someone to build the database system. He could do that in a weekend.

Every post is:

  • Actually near him

  • Actually actionable

  • Actually making something better

  • Something he could genuinely help with

It's 11 PM. He's been browsing for twenty minutes. He doesn't feel worse. He feels... energized? Like things are actually happening and he could be part of them?

The Stew

He clicks on the community garden post. A small icon appears: "Your Steward has some suggestions."

A chat window opens: "Hey! I noticed you're looking at the garden project. Your profile shows graphic design skills—this is a great match! They specifically need someone who can create visualizations for their grant proposal. Would you like me to introduce you to the project coordinator, or would you prefer to look around first?"

It's not pushy. It's... helpful. Like having a friend who actually knows what you're good at and connects you with opportunities.

"The Stew" is what people call it—the AI Steward. But unlike every other algorithm, this one:

  • Isn't trying to keep you scrolling

  • Isn't showing you content to make you angry

  • Isn't manipulating your emotions for engagement

  • Is transparent about what it's doing and why

Marcus asks: "What makes this project a good match?"

The Stew responds: "Based on your skills (graphic design, data visualization), your stated interests (community development, environmental projects), and your location (0.3 miles from the garden site). The project needs exactly what you offer. Also—15 people have already contributed to different objectives, so you'd be joining active momentum."

That's... actually relevant. Not "you looked at a plant once so here's 1000 plant ads." Actual intelligent matching.

Choosing What Matters

Marcus explores the settings. He can customize everything:

Priority Rankings:

  • How heavily to weight location (he sets it high—he wants local impact)

  • How much to emphasize skill matches (high—he wants to actually help)

  • Whether to show projects needing urgent help or long-term efforts (mix of both)

  • How much to surface new projects vs. established ones (he wants both)

Content Filters: He can choose to see or hide:

  • Debate/controversial topics (he sets to "occasional"—he wants some challenge but not constant arguing)

  • Funding requests (yes, he's interested in what needs support)

  • Educational content (yes, he wants to learn)

  • Success stories (definitely yes—he needs hope)

Tone Settings: This one's interesting. He can set the overall vibe:

  • Serious/professional

  • Casual/friendly

  • Inspiring/motivational

  • Direct/factual

He picks casual/inspiring. The Stew adjusts how it presents information accordingly.

What He Doesn't See

Here's what never appears in his feed:

  • Rage bait designed to make him angry

  • Engagement bait fishing for comments

  • Misinformation (the Stew flags disputed claims with note tokens linking to corrections)

  • Ads for products he doesn't need

  • Algorithmic manipulation optimizing for his worst impulses

  • Content from people trying to game the system for attention

Not because it's censored—but because he chose not to prioritize it. If he wants to see debates or controversial topics, he can. But the default isn't "maximize engagement by any means necessary."

The Community Algorithm Option

A week in, Marcus notices something called "Community Algorithm Proposals."

People are creating and sharing custom algorithms:

"The Builder" - Prioritizes projects in construction/implementation phase needing hands-on help "The Connector" - Surfaces projects needing coordination between different groups
"The Fixer" - Shows problems where small interventions create big impact
"The Learner" - Heavy on educational content and expert explanations
"The Organizer" - Emphasizes projects needing structure, planning, mobilization

Users vote on these. Rate them. Improve them. The best ones rise up. People can mix and match elements.

Marcus tries "The Builder" for a few days. His feed fills with hands-on projects needing immediate help. Tool library builds. Garden construction. Shelter renovations. Concrete stuff.

He likes it. He switches his feed to 60% Builder, 40% his custom settings.

The algorithm serves him. Not the other way around.

The First Project

He messages the community garden coordinator through the task card. She responds within an hour: "We'd love your help! The grant is due in three weeks. Can we meet this weekend to discuss what we need?"

They meet. She shows him the garden plan—it's ambitious. Converting an abandoned lot into a productive garden with educational programming for kids. They need visual materials for the grant: site plan, projected impact, community benefit breakdown.

He can do this. Actually wants to do this.

He creates the graphics over two weekends. They're good—better than he's done for his actual job lately because he actually cares about this.

The grant gets approved. The garden gets funded. Construction starts a month later.

And Marcus is there. Because the Stew notified him when the "Begin Construction" objective got activated. He shows up with a shovel. So do 30 other people who've been following the project.

They break ground together.

The Difference

Three months in, Marcus checks his screen time comparison:

Old social media:

  • Average 3 hours/day scrolling

  • Felt worse after using it

  • Learned nothing useful

  • Took zero action on anything

  • Made zero real connections

Needpedia:

  • Average 45 minutes/day

  • Feels energized after using it

  • Learned about 6 local projects, 3 policy issues, multiple skills

  • Volunteered for 4 projects, completed 8 task cards

  • Met 12 people in person, actually becoming friends with 3

The content is:

  • Relevant to his life

  • Actionable

  • Transparent about sourcing

  • Connected to real outcomes

  • Not designed to manipulate his emotions

His mental health is noticeably better. He's less anxious. Less angry. Less isolated. More connected to his community. More hopeful.

Not because he's avoiding problems—he's just seeing them in the context of people actively solving them instead of just screaming about them.

The Stew Gets Smarter

The Stew learns from his choices:

When he clicks "interested" on the tool library project, it notes that he likes systems-building initiatives with clear community benefit.

When he completes graphic design tasks successfully, it surfaces more opportunities matching those skills.

When he ignores posts about city-wide policy debates but engages with neighborhood-level projects, it adjusts the balance.

But here's the difference from other algorithms: he can see what it's learning and why. There's a transparency dashboard showing:

  • What patterns it's detecting in his behavior

  • How it's adjusting his feed

  • Why it's recommending specific content

He can correct it. "Actually, I am interested in policy—just at the local level, not national." The Stew adjusts.

It's AI, but it's serving him. The transparency makes all the difference.

Creating His Own Algorithm

Six months in, Marcus has strong opinions about what makes a good feed. He creates his own algorithm template: "The Pragmatic Local."

His settings:

  • Heavy emphasis on neighborhood-level (0-2 mile radius) projects

  • Priority on projects in implementation phase (not just planning)

  • Surface tasks requiring 2-6 hours commitment (he can't do huge time investments)

  • Show diverse project types (he wants variety)

  • Include some educational content on urban planning and community development (he's learning)

  • Minimal national/global issues unless connected to local action

He publishes it for others to try. 20 people adopt it in the first week. Several suggest improvements: "Add a filter for weekend-only opportunities" and "Include projects that need specific professional skills."

He incorporates the feedback. The algorithm gets better.

Other people in his city start using "The Pragmatic Local." It becomes the default template for people wanting to help locally without getting overwhelmed.

This is what user-created algorithms look like: actual humans designing feeds that serve human needs, not corporate engagement metrics.

The Feed He Never Knew He Wanted

His current feed shows:

  • The garden he helped launch (now producing food for 30 families)

  • A bike lane advocacy campaign he joined (city council vote next month)

  • The tool library system he built (200 users now)

  • A literacy program where he volunteers (teaching design skills to kids)

  • An upcoming community meeting about affordable housing

  • Someone needing help moving equipment for a neighborhood cleanup

  • A discussion about improving the local bus route (with transit planner explaining options)

  • Updates from other projects he follows

Every single item either:

  • Celebrates something good that happened

  • Shows progress on something in motion

  • Offers a concrete way to help

  • Teaches him something useful

  • Connects him to his community

Zero rage bait. Zero manipulation. Zero doomscrolling.

Just: here's what's happening, here's what's needed, here's how you can help if you want.

The Mental Health Shift

Marcus's therapist notices the change: "You seem more engaged. Less anxious about the state of the world."

"I'm still aware of problems," Marcus says. "But now I see them in the context of people actually working on them. It's not just doom—it's doom plus people doing something. That changes everything."

The algorithm he chose shows him:

  • Problems exist (reality)

  • People are addressing them (agency)

  • He can contribute (empowerment)

  • Progress is happening (hope)

Traditional social media showed him:

  • Problems exist (despair)

  • Nobody's doing anything (powerlessness)

  • You should be angry (manipulation)

  • Here's more problems (engagement trap)

Same problems. Different frame. Completely different mental health outcome.

The Contrast

His friend still uses traditional social media: "How do you stay informed?"

Marcus laughs. "I'm more informed than ever. I know what's happening in my actual community, which policies affect me, which projects need help. I'm less informed about celebrities, national outrage cycles, and things I can't affect. Turns out that trade-off is excellent for my mental health."

"But don't you feel like you're in a bubble?"

"Dude, you spent two hours yesterday watching people argue about something that happened in another state three years ago. Who's in a bubble?"

The Spread

Marcus's friends notice he's different. More energized. Actually doing stuff. Talks about real people he's met, real projects making tangible differences.

Five of them join Needpedia. They each customize their feeds differently:

  • One wants heavy educational content (learning mode)

  • One wants artistic/cultural projects (creative focus)

  • One wants environmental action (specific cause)

  • One wants to mentor youth (experience sharing)

  • One wants policy change (systemic focus)

They're all on the same platform but seeing completely different content. Because the algorithm serves their interests instead of forcing them into the same engagement-optimized feed.

The Realization

Marcus hasn't doomscrolled in six months.

He still checks social media—but now it's purposeful. "What needs help today? What progress happened? Who's doing interesting work?"

The Stew makes it easy: "Based on your available time today (you usually have Wednesday evenings free), here are three opportunities that match your skills and interests. No pressure—just letting you know they exist."

That's what an algorithm should do: help you be the person you want to be, not manipulate you into someone who makes them more money.

The Model

What traditional social media does:

  • Study your psychology to find your weaknesses

  • Show you content that triggers negative emotions (anger, envy, fear)

  • Keep you scrolling through engagement optimization

  • Sell your attention to advertisers

  • Make your mental health worse because worse = more engagement

  • Give you zero control over what you see

  • Hide how the algorithm works

What Needpedia does:

  • Let you define what you want to see

  • Show you actionable content connected to real outcomes

  • Help you use your time meaningfully with the Stew's assistance

  • Serve you (non-profit, no advertisers)

  • Make your mental health better because that's literally the goal

  • Give you complete control and transparency

  • Let you create and share your own algorithms

The business model determines everything. When users are the customers instead of the product being sold, the incentives align with their wellbeing.

The Future He's Building

Marcus is part of a community now. Real people working on real things. The algorithm connected him to:

  • His graphic design skills being useful

  • Projects that match his values

  • People in his neighborhood actually doing something

  • Opportunities to learn and grow

  • Tangible progress he can see

  • Purpose and connection

He's still online. Still using social media. But it's serving him instead of exploiting him.

That's what the algorithm you actually want looks like.

The Choice:

Keep using platforms that study your psychology to manipulate you into engagement that makes you miserable so they can sell ads...

Or switch to a platform where you choose what you see, the Stew helps you find meaningful opportunities, and the algorithm actually serves your interests because you're not the product being sold.

The technology exists. The infrastructure is being built. The only question is whether you're ready to take back your feed.
